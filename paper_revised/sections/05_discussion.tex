\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Parser Compliance and Structural Similarity Diverge}

Nearly all generated files are valid Gherkin (255 of 258), consistent with prior work showing that examples reduce syntax errors~\cite{karpurapu2024} and with industrial studies reporting high syntactic correctness of generated Gherkin~\cite{fonseca2025} and test code~\cite{ferreira2025}; the prompt ablation shows that this does not require the example (Section~\ref{sec:res-h2}). Matching the reference structure is harder, and the per-project results locate the difficulty: Fineract files are long and data-driven, and 94 of its 99 analysed references are cut by the encoder's 256-token limit, while Diaspora files depend on browser-level steps that a story does not state; the truncated story input of 84 Fineract files does not explain the gap (Section~\ref{sec:res-h1}). The experiment does not isolate these factors, but a pooled median that averages one project above the reference point with two below it is not a meaningful summary of capability. The similarity result is also more cautious than studies reporting strong semantic or text-similarity results~\cite{rathnayake2026,fernandes2025}, partly because this experiment uses a different metric, a fixed reference point, a multi-domain sample and a hypothesis test; cross-paper metric values are contextual comparisons, not model rankings.

\subsection{What the Similarity Metric Can and Cannot Show}

The ablation and the repeated runs show how fragile the RQ1 decision is: repeating the same prompt moves a fifth to a third of the 60 repeated files across 0.85. A different exemplar raises the median from 0.852 to 0.873, mostly in Fineract, but its paired effect stays within this run-to-run variation, while removing the example lowers the score by more than it. We conjecture that this follows from truncation: the encoder sees only the first 256 tokens of 94 of the 99 Fineract references, so an exemplar that shows a typical opening can raise the score without any evidence that the business content improved; the ablation does not test this.

Skeleton cosine is a measure of structural resemblance, not of correct behaviour. In the exploratory audit, 25 of 60 high-similarity files contain a defect that changes what the test checks, cosine did not rank defective files significantly differently, seeded defects leave the score at or above 0.8995, and changed numbers or values cannot change it at all. A threshold on this metric, whether 0.85 or any value between 0.80 and 0.90, therefore cannot certify that a generated file preserves its acceptance criteria, and high embedding similarity alone, in any of the studies of Table~\ref{tab:delta}, says little about it. We report 0.85 as a descriptive reference point and do not interpret files above it as correct. The overlap correlations in two projects (Section~\ref{sec:res-b1}) suggest that part of the similarity may also come from wording that the reverse-engineered story carries over from its reference; they do not establish a causal effect.

\subsection{Practical Use in BDD Workflows}

For stories of this kind, the evidence supports a bounded workflow: use GPT-5.4-mini to draft valid Gherkin, then have a reviewer check expected outcomes, conditions, data values and the coverage of each acceptance criterion. The audit suggests where review effort is needed most: wrong expected outcomes and missing verifying Then steps were the most frequent defects, and defective files were far more frequent in the data-heavy finance domain (16 of 20) than in the other two (6 and 3 of 20). The experiment does not justify promoting generated scenarios into CI/CD as authoritative acceptance tests.
