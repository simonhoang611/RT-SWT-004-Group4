\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Parser Compliance and Structural Similarity Diverge}

Nearly all generated files are valid Gherkin (255 of 258), so the model reliably follows the surface grammar. Same-project examples may contribute by showing keyword order, scenario structure, tables and local conventions, which is consistent with prior work showing that examples reduce syntax errors~\cite{karpurapu2024} and with industrial studies reporting high syntactic correctness of generated Gherkin~\cite{fonseca2025} and of generated test code~\cite{ferreira2025}. The prompt ablation (Section~\ref{sec:res-h2}) shows that this result does not require the example: in the same-time rerun 252 zero-shot outputs parse against 255 one-shot outputs of 255, and alternative exemplars give 254. The McNemar tests found no difference, although they cannot establish equivalence.

Matching the reference structure is harder, and the per-project results locate the difficulty. Fineract files are long, data-driven and full of dates, rates and balances, and 94 of its 99 analysed references are cut by the encoder's 256-token limit. The truncated story input of 84 Fineract files does not explain the gap, because these files score no lower than the other Fineract files (Section~\ref{sec:res-h1}); its effect on the content of the stories was not measured. Diaspora files depend on browser-level steps and selectors that a story does not state. Sylius checkout and cart scenarios are shorter and closer to their stories. The experiment does not isolate the causal contribution of the remaining factors, but it shows that a single pooled median, which averages one project above the reference point with two below it, is not a meaningful summary of capability across these projects.

\subsection{What the Similarity Metric Can and Cannot Show}

The ablation and the repeated runs also show how fragile the RQ1 decision is. So many files lie near 0.85 that repeating the same prompt moves a fifth to a third of the 60 repeated files across it. A different exemplar per project raises the median from 0.852 to 0.873, but its paired effect stays within this run-to-run variation, while removing the example lowers the score by more than it. Most of the exemplar-related change occurs in Fineract. We conjecture that this follows from truncation: the encoder sees only the first 256 tokens of 94 of the 99 Fineract references, so the opening of a file, that is its Background and first steps (the skeleton drops all table rows), largely determines the score, and an exemplar that shows a typical opening can raise the score without any evidence that the business content improved. The ablation does not test this explanation.

The revision analyses show that skeleton cosine is a measure of structural resemblance, not of correct behaviour. In the exploratory audit of the high-similarity range, 25 of 60 files contain a defect that changes what the test checks, and in this restricted sample cosine did not rank defective files significantly differently from non-defective ones. Nor does correct keyword order imply correct content. Seeded defects at the scenario level, including dropped Then steps and reordered steps, leave the score at or above 0.8995, and changed numbers or values cannot change it at all because the skeleton masks them. A threshold on this metric, whether 0.85 or any value between 0.80 and 0.90, therefore cannot certify that a generated file preserves its acceptance criteria. We report 0.85 as a descriptive reference point for structural similarity, together with its sensitivity analysis, and do not interpret files above it as correct. The overlap correlations in two projects (Section~\ref{sec:res-b1}) suggest that part of the similarity may also come from wording that the reverse-engineered story carries over from its reference; they do not establish a causal effect.

\subsection{Practical Use in BDD Workflows}

For stories of this kind, the evidence supports a bounded workflow: use GPT-5.4-mini to draft valid Gherkin, then have a reviewer check expected outcomes, conditions, data values and the coverage of each acceptance criterion. The audit suggests where review effort is needed most: wrong expected outcomes and missing verifying Then steps were the most frequent defects, and defective files were far more frequent in the data-heavy finance domain (16 of 20) than in the other two (6 and 3 of 20). The experiment does not justify promoting generated scenarios into CI/CD as authoritative acceptance tests. A parser pass establishes only that a file is valid Gherkin, and a high similarity score does not establish correctness.

\subsection{Relationship to Prior Work}

The findings refine, rather than contradict, earlier BDD-generation studies. High parser compliance aligns with the syntactic success reported in industrial pipelines, for generated Gherkin~\cite{fonseca2025} and for generated Cypress code~\cite{ferreira2025}, and with the lower syntax-error counts under few-shot prompting reported by Karpurapu et al.~\cite{karpurapu2024}. The similarity result is more cautious than studies reporting strong semantic or text-similarity results~\cite{rathnayake2026,fernandes2025}, partly because this experiment uses a different metric, a fixed reference point, a multi-domain sample and a hypothesis test against it. Our defect audit also suggests that high embedding similarity alone, in any of these studies, says little about whether acceptance criteria are preserved. Cross-paper metric values are therefore contextual comparisons, not model rankings.
