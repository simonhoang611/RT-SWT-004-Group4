\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LLM-Based Gherkin and BDD Generation}

Research on LLM-assisted BDD generation has typically emphasized either semantic resemblance or syntactic correctness. Rathnayake et al.~\cite{rathnayake2026} evaluated GPT-4, Claude 3 and Gemini on 500 user stories from four proprietary products of one company and reported strong BERTScore performance. They released the dataset, but all of its stories come from a single organization. Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2025} compared seven LLMs under zero-, one- and few-shot prompting on ten test descriptions from a single production system, scoring outputs with METEOR over five repetitions; their qualitative analysis found business-logic flaws, such as a reversed or omitted outcome, among the most frequent errors. Karpurapu et al.~\cite{karpurapu2024} evaluated GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama-2-13B, and PaLM-2 and found fewer Gherkin-lint syntax errors with few-shot prompting overall; zero-shot outputs accounted for 89\% of the observed syntax errors. This supports evaluating example-based prompting, while noting that their validator was a linter rather than a runtime parser.

Industrial pipeline studies provide a complementary view. Ferreira et al.~\cite{ferreira2025} evaluated GPT-4 Turbo in an industrial web-application workflow; all 50 Cypress test cases from their downstream Test Flow step were valid TypeScript, yet a manual analysis found that only 60\% reflected their Gherkin scenarios. Fonseca et al.~\cite{fonseca2025} evaluated the AToMIC framework on 13 issues from the BMW MyBMW application and, by a manual keyword check, found 93.3\% of the generated Gherkin scenarios syntactically valid. These results demonstrate that LLMs can be integrated into practical workflows, but single-organization or single-application evaluations make cross-domain generalization difficult to assess.

\subsection{Test Generation and Semantic Evaluation}

Broader software-test generation research shows that syntactic success and fault-revealing or semantic quality should not be conflated. TestBench provides a benchmark for evaluating class-level test-case generation across LLMs~\cite{zhang2025testbench}. Chang and Shokrolah Shirazi propose a systematic benchmark for assessing LLM test-case generation over generated program structures~\cite{chang2025}. XUAT-Copilot uses multiple LLM-based agents for automated user-acceptance testing of WeChat Pay~\cite{wang2024xuat}. For generated code, EvalPlus found pass@$k$ up to 19.3--28.9\% lower once the HumanEval tests were extended 80-fold~\cite{liu2023evalplus}, and CodeJudge shows that token- and embedding-based metrics fail on code that is semantically equivalent to the reference but written differently~\cite{tong2024codejudge}. Where an executable oracle exists it replaces resemblance, as in LIBRO's criterion that a test fail on the buggy version and pass on the fixed one~\cite{kang2023libro}, TiCoder's use of tests to clarify user intent~\cite{fakhoury2024ticoder}, and Meta's mutant-guided ACH~\cite{foster2025ach}. Gherkin generation has no such oracle, because a valid scenario can still state the wrong business rule.

For comparison with a reference, sentence embeddings provide a scalable alternative to manual labeling. Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019} enables dense representations suitable for cosine similarity. However, concrete names and values in raw Gherkin can dominate textual resemblance without representing domain intent. The present study therefore masks quoted values and numbers and keeps only keyword lines before embedding, and it treats the resulting skeleton similarity as a proxy rather than a definitive measure of business correctness. Section~\ref{sec:res-defects} tests how well that proxy tracks business-logic defects.

Reporting is a further concern. A review of 74 LLM studies in requirements engineering found that only 16\% used public datasets and 39\% did not state how prompts were selected~\cite{zadenoori2025}. Guidelines for empirical software-engineering studies with LLMs require reporting the model version, configuration and date of use, publishing all prompts and explaining how few-shot examples were selected, and they name sensitivity to in-context examples as a validity threat~\cite{baltes2026guidelines}.

\textbf{Contribution 1, the benchmark.} The other four studies with named sources each draw on one company, system or application, and Ferreira et al.\ and Fonseca et al.\ do not release their data; Karpurapu et al.\ use about 50 stories whose domains are not named. Our 261 reference files come from three public projects in different domains and are released with their construction and a measurement of its leakage.

\textbf{Contribution 2, the evaluation.} Karpurapu et al.\ check syntax with a linter, and the other studies measure semantics with similarity metrics, ratings or an LLM validator. Fernandes et al.\ describe business-logic flaws qualitatively, but none of the five studies tests whether its semantic measure reacts to business-logic defects. Our defect audit and perturbation test show that files with high skeleton similarity can still violate their acceptance criteria. Three of the studies compare prompting techniques, but none varies which example is given in the prompt; our ablation shows that removing the example lowers skeleton similarity by more than repeated runs vary, while a different exemplar moves single files in both directions without a detectable average effect. The behave parser answers only whether a generated file is valid Gherkin, and skeleton cosine similarity estimates closeness to the reference, so the two outcomes are reported and interpreted separately.

\textbf{Contribution 3, the replication package.} It contains the model identifier, prompts, scripts, API logs, generated outputs and all revision analyses, so that every number in this paper can be recomputed.
