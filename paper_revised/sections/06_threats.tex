\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Internal Validity}

\textbf{Reverse-engineered stories.} The user stories were written by GPT-5.4-mini from the reference files, so the input can carry wording, structure and scenario order from the very file it is compared with. The model that wrote the stories is also the model under test. Story--reference trigram overlap is low (median 4.9\%) but correlates with similarity in two of the three projects (Section~\ref{sec:res-b1}), which suggests that part of the measured similarity comes from this construction. Conversely, 84 Fineract stories were written from only the first 15,000 characters of a longer reference, so they cannot describe the rest of it, and the full reference is still used for comparison. Our trigram measure captures only lexical leakage, not structural leakage such as the order and number of criteria. No independently written backlog was available, so all results apply to reverse-engineered stories and may overstate performance on natural requirements.

\textbf{Exemplars and pilot.} The three original exemplars were part of the dataset; we exclude them from the primary analysis and report both denominators. The 30 pilot stories used when the threshold was set are part of the evaluated set.

\textbf{Prompt ablation.} C2 uses one random draw of exemplars; other draws may give other results. The ablation runs took place three months after C0, so a change in the hosted service cannot be excluded, although the API returned the requested snapshot name for every C1 and C2 call; the C0 log records only the requested name.

\textbf{Hosted model.} The serving infrastructure is outside our control. Pinning the snapshot, using temperature 0 and logging each call reduce but do not remove nondeterminism. The C0 log records the requested rather than the returned model name.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

\textbf{Similarity metric.} Skeleton cosine is an imperfect proxy for business correctness. It is blind to numbers and quoted values by construction, ignores data tables and Examples, and for about two thirds of the files it sees only the first 256 tokens of a skeleton. Within the audited high-similarity files it did not separate defective from non-defective outputs significantly, and scenario-level seeded defects barely changed it. Conversely, a valid alternative scenario can score low because it differs from the single expert reference.

\textbf{Defect labels.} The 60 audit labels were drafted by an AI assistant and retained unchanged by a reviewing author, whose review was recorded as one confirmation rather than per-file decisions. Agreement with an independent human rater on 15 files (independence rests on the rater's own declaration) was $\kappa = 0.571$ (business logic) and $\kappa = 0.609$ (temporal/causal), below the 0.7 our protocol required. The protocol required a consensus round and relabelling in that case; we did not perform them, and we report all labels and both $\kappa$ values unchanged. With 15 files $\kappa$ is imprecise, and the disagreements suggest that the reviewed labels may undercount defects in the social-network domain. The defect rates should be read as indicative. The two label facets are not independent measurements, and the audit covers only files at or above 0.85.

\textbf{Perturbation test.} The test mutates single extracted scenarios, omitted the Background of 74 of 120 source scenarios, and draws several mutants from the same scenario, so its pairs are not independent and do not estimate whole-file sensitivity.

\textbf{Parser compliance.} A parser pass shows only that a file is valid Gherkin. It does not show successful execution against the application or correct step implementations. The submitted version's dry-run check did not measure even this, which we corrected.

\subsection{External and Conclusion Validity}

The dataset spans three mature, English-language public projects and is not representative of BDD practice. Results may differ for short tickets, mixed-language requirements, private conventions or projects with unstable step definitions. The projects contribute unequal numbers of files (99, 99 and 60 after exclusion), and they differ significantly in similarity, so pooled results do not generalise across domains.

Neither threshold is an industry standard. The 0.85 similarity threshold was fixed in the proposal without calibration and the RQ1 conclusion changes between 0.84 and 0.85. All analyses added in revision were planned after the reviews and are exploratory; their $p$-values are not corrected across analyses. The proposal planned a 1,500-token output cap that the runs did not apply, which may have affected output length, cost and both outcome measures; no capped run was made for comparison.
